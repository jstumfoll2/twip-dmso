\section{MODEL OF A DIRECT CURRENT MOTOR}
\label{sec:motor}

The armature circuit of a brushed DC motor with terminal voltage $V_a$,
resistance $R$, inductance $L$, and back-EMF constant $k_e$ obeys Kirchhoff's
voltage law \cite{desilva2004mechatronics},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:circuit}
L\frac{\dd i}{\dd t}+Ri+k_e\omega=V_a ,
\end{equation}
where $i$ is the armature current and $\omega=\dot\vartheta$ the shaft rate.
Rearranging,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:circuit2}
\frac{\dd i}{\dd t}=\frac{V_a}{L}-\frac{R}{L}i-\frac{k_e}{L}\omega .
\end{equation}
The electromagnetic torque developed is proportional to the current,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:torque}
\tau_m=k_mi ,
\end{equation}
and the viscous friction torque is proportional to the shaft rate,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:friction}
\tau_f=k_f\omega .
\end{equation}
Applying Newton's second law and summing moments about the motor output shaft
against an applied load torque $\tau_a$ and load inertia $I_m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shaft}
I_m\dot\omega=\tau_m-\tau_f-\tau_a .
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:torque} and \eqref{eq:friction} into \eqref{eq:shaft} and
rearranging gives the second equation of motion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shaft2}
\frac{\dd\omega}{\dd t}=\frac{k_m}{I_m}i-\frac{k_f}{I_m}\omega-\frac{\tau_a}{I_m} .
\end{equation}

Equations \eqref{eq:circuit2} and \eqref{eq:shaft2} describe the fundamental
behavior of a DC motor as a pair of first-order differential equations relating
circuit current, motor speed, and applied torque. For a balancing robot a
simplified model provides acceptable performance. Assuming the motor inductance
$L$ and the friction coefficient $k_f$ are negligible,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isimple}
i=\frac{V_a}{R}-\frac{k_e}{R}\omega ,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wsimple}
\frac{\dd\omega}{\dd t}=\frac{k_m}{I_m}i-\frac{\tau_a}{I_m} .
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:isimple} into \eqref{eq:wsimple} gives a model that does
not include the circuit current,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:motor}
\frac{\dd\omega}{\dd t}
=-\frac{k_mk_e}{I_mR}\omega+\frac{k_m}{I_mR}V_a-\frac{\tau_a}{I_m} .
\end{equation}
This elimination is desirable: the current is not directly commandable on the
physical system and would add a state with no observable benefit. Neglecting the
inductance amounts to assuming the current reaches steady state immediately
relative to the motor speed, which is justified because the electrical time
constant $L/R$ is orders of magnitude shorter than the mechanical one. In
state-space form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:motorss}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
\begin{bmatrix}\vartheta\\\omega\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1\\[4pt]
0 & -\dfrac{k_mk_e}{I_mR}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\vartheta\\\omega\end{bmatrix}
+\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0\\[4pt]
\dfrac{k_m}{I_mR} & -\dfrac{1}{I_m}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}V_a\\\tau_a\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\section{TWO WHEELED INVERTED PENDULUM DYNAMIC MODEL}
\label{sec:twipmodel}

The TWIP shares many similarities with the pendulum-on-a-cart problem. The
motion is comparable but the dynamics are more involved. The analysis begins
with the two wheels, the pendulum is analyzed separately, and the two sets of
equations are combined by eliminating the interface reactions, yielding two
dynamic equations describing the motion of the system.

\subsection{Wheel Dynamics}

The free-body diagram of the left wheel is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fbdwheel}.
The reaction forces between the wheel and the pendulum are $P_L$ and $H_L$, the
torque applied to the wheel by the DC motor is $C_L$, and the friction force
between the ground and the wheel is $H_{fL}$. The equations for the left and
right wheels are completely analogous, so only the left wheel is analyzed. The
wheels are assumed to remain in ground contact at all times with no slip.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{fbd_wheel}
\caption{Left wheel free body diagram.}
\label{fig:fbdwheel}
\end{figure}

Summing forces in the $x$ direction,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wheelx}
M_w\ddot x=H_{fL}-H_L .
\end{equation}
Summing moments about the center of the wheel,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wheelm}
I_w\ddot\theta_w=C_L-H_{fL}r ,
\end{equation}
where $I_w$ is the wheel moment of inertia and $\ddot\theta_w$ the wheel angular
acceleration. \rev{The wheel angle $\theta_w$ and the motor torque $C_L$ are both taken
clockwise, the sense in which a wheel turns when the robot rolls in the positive $x$
direction. Equations \eqref{eq:wheelm}, \eqref{eq:noslip} and the back-EMF term of
\eqref{eq:CL} require this; the $\theta_w$ arrow in Figure~\ref{fig:fbdwheel} is
drawn the other way.} Starting from the motor shaft equation \eqref{eq:shaft} and
substituting \eqref{eq:isimple}, the motor torque delivered to either wheel is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CL}
C_L=C_R=\tau_m=-\frac{k_mk_e}{R}\dot\theta_w+\frac{k_m}{R}V_a .
\end{equation}
\rev{In \eqref{eq:CL}, $\dot\theta_w$ must be read as the wheel rate relative to
the motor stator, which is fixed to the pendulum body. The development below
follows revision~7 in substituting the absolute rate $\dot x/r$, which drops a
tilt-rate term; the correction used for the results is given in
Section~\ref{sec:simmodel}.}
Using \eqref{eq:CL} in \eqref{eq:wheelm} and rearranging gives the friction
reaction force,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:HfL}
H_{fL}=-\frac{k_mk_e}{Rr}\dot\theta_w+\frac{k_m}{Rr}V_a
-\frac{I_w}{r}\ddot\theta_w .
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:HfL} into \eqref{eq:wheelx} gives the equation of motion
for the left wheel,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wheelL}
M_w\ddot x=-\frac{k_mk_e}{Rr}\dot\theta_w+\frac{k_m}{Rr}V
-\frac{I_w}{r}\ddot\theta_w-H_L ,
\end{equation}
and an identical development for the right wheel gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wheelR}
M_w\ddot x=-\frac{k_mk_e}{Rr}\dot\theta_w+\frac{k_m}{Rr}V
-\frac{I_w}{r}\ddot\theta_w-H_R .
\end{equation}

The no-slip assumption provides the transformation between rotational and
linear motion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noslip}
\ddot\theta_w=\frac{\ddot x}{r},\qquad \dot\theta_w=\frac{\dot x}{r} .
\end{equation}
Applying \eqref{eq:noslip} to \eqref{eq:wheelL} and \eqref{eq:wheelR},
\begin{align}
M_w\ddot x&=-\frac{k_mk_e}{Rr^2}\dot x+\frac{k_m}{Rr}V
-\frac{I_w}{r^2}\ddot x-H_L ,\label{eq:wheelL2}\\[4pt]
M_w\ddot x&=-\frac{k_mk_e}{Rr^2}\dot x+\frac{k_m}{Rr}V
-\frac{I_w}{r^2}\ddot x-H_R .\label{eq:wheelR2}
\end{align}
Adding \eqref{eq:wheelL2} and \eqref{eq:wheelR2} gives the equation of motion
for the combined wheel system,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wheelcomb}
2\left(M_w+\frac{I_w}{r^2}\right)\ddot x
=-\frac{2k_mk_e}{Rr^2}\dot x+\frac{2k_m}{Rr}V-\bigl(H_L+H_R\bigr).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Pendulum Dynamics}

Figure~\ref{fig:fbdpend} shows the free-body diagram of the inverted pendulum.
Two reference frames are used: an inertial frame attached to the base of the
pendulum, and a translating frame at the center of gravity. Here $l$ is the
distance from the base to the center of gravity; $\alpha_p$, $\omega_p$, and
$\theta$ are the angular acceleration, angular velocity, and angular position of
the pendulum; $x,\dot x,\ddot x$ are the position, velocity, and acceleration of
the pendulum base; and $x_p,\dot x_p,\ddot x_p$ those of the center of gravity.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{fbd_pendulum}
\caption{Inverted pendulum free body diagram.}
\label{fig:fbdpend}
\end{figure}

Summing forces in the $x_p$ direction,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pendx}
\sum F_{xp}=H_L+H_R=M_p\ddot x_p ,
\end{equation}
and in the $y_p$ direction,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pendy}
\sum F_{yp}=P_L+P_R-M_pg=M_p\ddot y_p .
\end{equation}

The acceleration of a point in a translating frame follows from relative motion
analysis \cite{hibbeler2010dynamics}. The acceleration of the pendulum center of
gravity, denoted $\mathbf{a}_B$, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relmotion}
\mathbf{a}_B=\mathbf{a}_A
+\bm{\alpha}_p\times\mathbf{r}_{B/A}
+\bm{\omega}_p\times\bigl(\bm{\omega}_p\times\mathbf{r}_{B/A}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
\mathbf{a}_B&=\ddot x_p\hat\imath+\ddot y_p\hat\jmath ,\label{eq:aB}\\
\mathbf{r}_{B/A}&=l\sin\theta\,\hat\imath-l\cos\theta\,\hat\jmath ,\label{eq:rBA}\\
\bm{\omega}_p&=\dot\theta\,\hat k ,\label{eq:omegap}\\
\bm{\alpha}_p&=\ddot\theta\,\hat k ,\label{eq:alphap}\\
\mathbf{a}_A&=\ddot x\,\hat\imath .\label{eq:aA}
\end{align}
Using \eqref{eq:rBA}--\eqref{eq:aA} in \eqref{eq:relmotion},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aBfull}
\mathbf{a}_B=\Bigl(\ddot x+\ddot\theta l\cos\theta-\dot\theta^2l\sin\theta\Bigr)\hat\imath
+\Bigl(\ddot\theta l\sin\theta+\dot\theta^2l\cos\theta\Bigr)\hat\jmath .
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:aBfull} into \eqref{eq:pendx} and \eqref{eq:pendy},
\begin{align}
H_L+H_R&=M_p\Bigl(\ddot x+\ddot\theta l\cos\theta-\dot\theta^2l\sin\theta\Bigr),
\label{eq:Hsum}\\[4pt]
P_L+P_R&=M_pg+M_p\Bigl(\ddot\theta l\sin\theta+\dot\theta^2l\cos\theta\Bigr).
\label{eq:Psum}
\end{align}

Summing moments about the pendulum center of gravity, with the
\rev{counterclockwise direction (the sense of $\theta$, since
$\bm{\omega}_p=\dot\theta\,\hat k$) taken as positive,}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pendm}
\sum M=\bigl(C_L+C_R\bigr)-\bigl(H_L+H_R\bigr)l\cos\theta
-\bigl(P_L+P_R\bigr)l\sin\theta=I_p\ddot\theta .
\end{equation}
\rev{The motors are mounted on the pendulum, so the torque $C_L+C_R$ that turns the
wheels clockwise reacts on the pendulum counterclockwise, with positive sign.
Revision~7 entered this reaction with a negative sign and described the sum as
clockwise-positive, while the $H$ and $P$ terms and $I_p\ddot\theta$ are written
counterclockwise-positive. That error reverses the motor's reaction on the body;
it propagates to \eqref{eq:nl1}, \eqref{eq:lin1}, and the second and fourth rows
of \eqref{eq:linss}, all corrected below. Remark~\ref{rem:momentum} gives an
independent check.}

\subsection{Combined Equations of Motion}

Combining \eqref{eq:CL}, \eqref{eq:Hsum}, \eqref{eq:Psum}, and \eqref{eq:pendm},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:combined}
\begin{aligned}
\left(-\frac{2k_mk_e}{R}\dot\theta_w+\frac{2k_m}{R}V_a\right)
&-\Bigl(M_p\bigl(\ddot x+\ddot\theta l\cos\theta-\dot\theta^2l\sin\theta\bigr)\Bigr)l\cos\theta\\
&-\Bigl(M_pg+M_p\bigl(\ddot\theta l\sin\theta+\dot\theta^2l\cos\theta\bigr)\Bigr)l\sin\theta
=I_p\ddot\theta .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Cancelling terms, rearranging, and applying the identity
$\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$ gives the first nonlinear equation of motion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nl1}
\bigl(I_p+M_pl^2\bigr)\ddot\theta
+\frac{2k_mk_e}{Rr}\dot x
-\frac{2k_m}{R}V
+M_pgl\sin\theta
=-M_pl\ddot x\cos\theta .
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:Hsum} to remove the body forces from \eqref{eq:wheelcomb} gives
the second nonlinear equation of motion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nl2}
\frac{2k_m}{Rr}V=\left(2M_w+\frac{2I_w}{r^2}+M_p\right)\ddot x
+\frac{2k_mk_e}{Rr^2}\dot x
+M_pl\ddot\theta\cos\theta
-M_pl\dot\theta^2\sin\theta .
\end{equation}

Equations \eqref{eq:nl1} and \eqref{eq:nl2} describe the full nonlinear motion
of the two-wheeled inverted pendulum.

\subsection{Linearization}

In the convention of \eqref{eq:rBA}, $\theta=\pi$ is the upright
vertical configuration (the angle drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:fbdpend} is measured
from the upward vertical, so it equals $\phi$ below). A set of linearized equations is obtained by writing
$\theta=\pi+\phi$, where $\phi$ is a small angle from the vertical. The
small-angle assumptions are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smallangle}
\cos\theta=-1,\qquad \sin\theta=-\phi,
\end{equation}
together with the assumption that the squared angular rate is small,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smallrate}
\dot\theta^2=0 .
\end{equation}
Applying \eqref{eq:smallangle} and \eqref{eq:smallrate} to \eqref{eq:nl1} and
\eqref{eq:nl2} yields the linearized equations of motion,
\begin{align}
\bigl(I_p+M_pl^2\bigr)\ddot\phi+\frac{2k_mk_e}{Rr}\dot x
-\frac{2k_m}{R}V-M_pgl\phi&=M_pl\ddot x ,\label{eq:lin1}\\[4pt]
\frac{2k_m}{Rr}V=\left(2M_w+\frac{2I_w}{r^2}+M_p\right)\ddot x
+\frac{2k_mk_e}{Rr^2}\dot x-M_pl\ddot\phi&. \label{eq:lin2}
\end{align}

\rev{\begin{remark}[Sign of the gravity term]\label{rem:gravsign}
Rearranging \eqref{eq:lin1} gives
$\bigl(I_p+M_pl^2\bigr)\ddot\phi=M_pgl\phi+\cdots$, so the gravitational term
enters with positive sign and is destabilizing. This is the expected signature
of an upright linearization and confirms the convention: $\phi$ is the
displacement from the \emph{unstable} equilibrium, not from the hanging one. It
is also consistent with \eqref{eq:linss}, whose $(2,3)$ and $(4,3)$ entries are
both positive. The description of $\theta=\pi$ in revision~7 as the
``statically stable vertical point'' is a mislabeling; the corrected reading is
given in Section~\ref{sec:simchecks}.
\end{remark}}

Rearranging \eqref{eq:lin1} and \eqref{eq:lin2} into state-space form
$\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathcal{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathcal{B}V$ with
$\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x&\dot x&\phi&\dot\phi\end{bmatrix}^T$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linss}
\mathcal{A}=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 & 0\\[4pt]
0 & -\dfrac{2k_mk_e\bigl(I_p+M_pl^2+M_plr\bigr)}{Rr^2\alpha}
  & \dfrac{M_p^2gl^2}{\alpha} & 0\\[8pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & 1\\[4pt]
0 & -\dfrac{2k_mk_e\bigl(r\beta+M_pl\bigr)}{Rr^2\alpha}
  & \dfrac{M_pgl\beta}{\alpha} & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathcal{B}=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\[4pt]
\dfrac{2k_m\bigl(I_p+M_pl^2+M_plr\bigr)}{Rr\alpha}\\[8pt]
0\\[4pt]
\dfrac{2k_m\bigl(r\beta+M_pl\bigr)}{Rr\alpha}
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta}
\beta:=2M_w+\frac{2I_w}{r^2}+M_p
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alphadef}
\alpha:=I_p\beta+2M_pl^2\left(M_w+\frac{I_w}{r^2}\right).
\end{equation}

\rev{\begin{remark}[Check on the input direction]\label{rem:momentum}
The motor torque acts between the pendulum and the wheels, so it cannot change
the angular momentum of the robot about the wheel contact point; only gravity,
which has no moment about that point when the robot is upright, and the motion
of the point itself can. At the upright equilibrium with zero rates, the
acceleration produced by the voltage alone must therefore leave that angular
momentum unchanged. Measured clockwise, the angular momentum about the contact
point is $(r\beta+M_pl)\dot x-(I_p+M_pl^2+M_plr)\dot\phi$ at $\phi=0$, and its
rate of change is zero only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:momentum}
\frac{\mathcal{B}_2}{\mathcal{B}_4}=\frac{I_p+M_pl^2+M_plr}{r\beta+M_pl} .
\end{equation}
The input vector of \eqref{eq:linss} satisfies \eqref{eq:momentum}, and the same
ratio relates $\mathcal{A}_{22}$ to $\mathcal{A}_{42}$, since the back-EMF torque
is internal too. Both entries of $\mathcal{B}$ are positive: a positive voltage
pushes the base forward and turns the body backward. The input vector of
revision~7, with $I_p+M_pl^2-M_plr$ and $M_pl-r\beta$ in place of the corrected
factors, fails this check.
\end{remark}}

For the development in Chapter~\ref{ch:control} it is convenient to name the
entries of \eqref{eq:linss} directly. Writing $x_1=x$, $x_2=\dot x$, $x_3=\phi$,
$x_4=\dot\phi$, $u=V$, and appending the unmodeled and parametric uncertainty as
$d_1$ and $d_2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:plantform}
\begin{aligned}
\dot x_1&=x_2,\\
\dot x_2&=A_1x_2+A_2x_3+B_1u+d_1(\mathbf{x}),\\
\dot x_3&=x_4,\\
\dot x_4&=A_3x_2+A_4x_3+B_2u+d_2(\mathbf{x}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $A_1,\dots,A_4,B_1,B_2$ read off from \eqref{eq:linss}: $A_1=\mathcal{A}_{22}$,
$A_2=\mathcal{A}_{23}$, $A_3=\mathcal{A}_{42}$, $A_4=\mathcal{A}_{43}$,
$B_1=\mathcal{B}_2$, $B_2=\mathcal{B}_4$. \rev{The tilt rate $x_4$ does not appear
because \eqref{eq:CL} was evaluated with the absolute wheel rate. With the
relative rate of Section~\ref{sec:simmodel} the terms $\mathcal{A}_{24}x_4$ and
$\mathcal{A}_{44}x_4$ join the second and fourth equations; the designs of
Chapter~\ref{ch:control} carry them as known terms, and the uncertainties $d_1$
and $d_2$ absorb whatever the nominal model leaves out. (Revision~7 stated
instead that $A_4=0$ in the nominal model; $A_4$ is the gravity coefficient
$\mathcal{A}_{43}$, which is not zero.)}

\rev{\begin{remark}[Structural consequences]
Two features of \eqref{eq:plantform} drive everything that follows. First,
$B_1$ and $B_2$ are both nonzero and the input is scalar, so the system is
underactuated and no choice of $u$ cancels both $d_1$ and $d_2$: the uncertainty
is unmatched. Second, $A_2\neq0$, so the tilt state enters the translational
acceleration directly. This is what makes $x_3$ usable as a virtual control for
the position subsystem, and it is also the term that couples the two error
subsystems in the backstepping design. It must be retained; see
Section~\ref{sec:cffb}.
\end{remark}}

\section{MODEL VALIDATION}
\label{sec:modelsim}

\rev{The model of Section~\ref{sec:twipmodel} is used three times in this
thesis: it sets the controller gains of Chapter~\ref{ch:control}, it is the
prediction model $F,G$ inside every observer, and it is the truth model of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. Revision~7 checked it only by simulating it. A
simulation shows that the equations are implemented as written, not that they
describe the robot, so this section tests the model against the robot's
recorded data. Each test is stated with the value every candidate model
predicts. A candidate is called contradicted by a test when its prediction
misses the measurement by several times the error of the best candidate, or,
for a fitted coefficient, lies well outside the bootstrap interval of the
measurement; by the second rule some measured coefficients contradict M1 as
well, and they are reported.}

\subsection{Candidate Models}
\label{sec:candidates}

\rev{Three candidates span the questions the derivation leaves open: the sign
of the motor reaction on the body, the motor constants, and the mass
properties. They share the mass matrix and differ in how the motor couples the
wheels to the body.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
\item[M1 (corrected).] Reaction $+(C_L+C_R)$ on the body, as in
\eqref{eq:pendm}; back-EMF on the wheel rate relative to the body,
\eqref{eq:relemf}; parameters of Section~\ref{sec:simmodel}. This is the model
behind the results of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.
\item[M2 (revision~7 sign).] Reaction $-(C_L+C_R)$, with the back-EMF on
$\dot x/r-\dot\phi$, the relative rate that makes $-T$ energy-consistent;
parameters of Section~\ref{sec:simmodel}. It differs from M1 only in the
disputed sign.
\item[M3 (revision~7).] Reaction $-(C_L+C_R)$, back-EMF on the absolute wheel
rate $\dot x/r$, and the values $k_e=\SI{0.00361}{V.s/rad}$ and
$I_p=\SI{0.025}{kg.m^2}$ used in revision~7.
\end{description}
Table~\ref{tab:candidates} lists the predictions used below. Both input gains
are positive in every candidate at the parameters used here, so a positive
voltage pushes the base forward and turns the body backward in all three. (For
M2 and M3, $\mathcal{B}_4$ changes sign below $l\approx\SI{0.06}{m}$, inside the
range searched in Section~\ref{sec:gaintest}; the M2 plants there fall with
either sign of the command.) The sign question therefore does not
show up as a reversed response. It shows up in the magnitudes: per volt, M1
turns the body seven to ten times faster than M2 and M3, and it moves the
base much less per unit of body rotation.}

\subsection{Simulation Checks}
\label{sec:simchecks}

The uncontrolled nonlinear equations \eqref{eq:nl1}--\eqref{eq:nl2} are
integrated from two initial conditions with the motor voltage held at zero.
\rev{In the convention of \eqref{eq:smallangle}, $\theta=\pi$ is the upright
configuration and $\theta=0$ the hanging one. Started at
$\theta=\SI{180}{\degree}$ with zero rate, the system remains there
(Figure~\ref{fig:stab180}). This confirms that the upright configuration is an
equilibrium of the implemented equations; it is not evidence of stability, and
as Remark~\ref{rem:gravsign} establishes, a perturbation of \SI{0.1}{\degree}
grows to \SI{10}{\degree} in \SI{0.88}{s}. Revision~7 described the two
equilibria in the opposite sense.

Started at $\theta=\SI{10}{\degree}$, the system oscillates about the hanging
equilibrium (Figure~\ref{fig:stab10}) with a period of \SI{0.84}{s}. The swing
is lightly damped, falling to about \SI{7}{\degree} in \SI{10}{s}, because the
back-EMF damps only the rotation of the wheels relative to the body, and in a
free swing the wheels roll nearly in step with it. The base moves initially in
the positive direction and oscillates between \SI{-1}{mm} and \SI{17}{mm}.

These checks would catch a sign or transcription error in the implemented
equations, but they are not validation. Table~\ref{tab:simchecks} shows that
all three candidates pass them, so they cannot decide between them.}

\subsection{Recorded Data and Sign Conventions}
\label{sec:datatests}

\rev{Four logs of the robot balancing under LQR control are analysed here:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{implementationtest1.txt} and \texttt{implementationtest2.txt}:
firmware v8, \SI{13}{ms} loop, \SI{3.0}{s} and \SI{3.9}{s} long, with the
potentiometer gains printed;
\item \texttt{implementationtest4.txt} and \texttt{implementationtest5.txt}:
firmware v9, \SI{10}{ms} loop, \SI{12.6}{s} and \SI{21.2}{s} long, gains not
printed.
\end{itemize}
They are called tests~1, 2, 4 and~5 below. Each row holds the raw
gyroscope rate, the raw accelerometer components, the encoder position $p$ and
its backward difference, the filtered tilt, the on-board estimates, and the
motor command in PWM counts (\num{5904.5} counts per volt). Other balancing
logs exist and were not analysed: 26 logs of firmware v7 in
\texttt{Tests/LQR Tests} (February 2015, on the present gearmotors and
encoders, most with tilt, position, accelerometer, gyroscope and command) and
\texttt{newmotortest1.txt}. They are a replication set for the tests below. No
free-swing, mass property or locked-rotor measurement exists; the motor sweeps
in \texttt{Tests/Motor Tests} were recorded on the gearmotors that firmware v7
replaced. The analysis is the script \texttt{scripts/validate\_plant.py} of the
accompanying code, which writes its results to
\texttt{generated/plant\_validation.json}.

Three rules apply throughout. The on-board estimates are not used as
measurements, because they were computed with the revision~7 matrices $F$ and
$G$; they are used only to recover the gains. The tilt is the integrated
gyroscope, started from the filtered tilt, because the accelerometer and the
complementary filter read tilt relative to the apparent vertical, which is
displaced by about $\ddot x/g$ whenever the robot accelerates. And the inertial
signals are advanced by \SI{7}{ms}, the estimated lag of the IMU behind the
encoders (the \SI{4.8}{ms} group delay of its \SI{42}{Hz} low-pass filter plus
sampling), in the v8 runs as well. Without this correction the ranking of the
candidates below is unchanged, but the fitted encoder coefficient $c_1$ of
tests~4 and~5 falls from 0.205 and 0.203 to 0.15 and 0.14.

\paragraph{Sign conventions.} Two relative signs must be fixed before the logs
can be compared with a model. The first is that of the command relative to the
tilt. The gains recovered in Section~\ref{sec:gaintest} feed the logged tilt
back with a positive gain ($+118$ in v9, $+27$ to $+73$ in v8), which
stabilizes the body only if the logged command and tilt have the signs of $V$
and $\phi$, because $\mathcal{B}_4>0$ in every candidate. The second is that of
the encoder relative to the tilt. Each v9 run starts with the robot leaning
(\SI{24.8}{\degree} in test~4, \SI{15.7}{\degree} in test~5) and the command
saturated at \SI{-10}{V}. Within \SI{100}{ms} the gyroscope shows the body
turning back toward upright at \SIrange{178}{193}{\degree/s} while the logged position rises
by \SI{51}{mm}, whereas every candidate, driven by the logged \SI{-10}{V},
predicts $p=x+r\phi$ to fall by 33 to \SI{58}{mm}. The same holds from
\SIrange{0.1}{0.8}{s}, after any contact with the support has ended: since
$c_1>0$ in every candidate, \eqref{eq:momenc} below requires $p$ to fall with
the tilt, and the encoder fit there gives $c_1=+0.20$ only if the logged
position is mirrored. So the logged position is mirrored: it equals $-p$. The
encoder tests below use this same transient, so their positive $c_1$ is not an
independent check of the mirror. The accelerometer is, because its sign is
fixed by gravity through the tilt: in this frame it gives an axle coefficient
of the same sign as the encoder (0.19 and 0.24, against 0.16 and 0.16 from the
encoder). The mirror is inferred from these two v9 transients alone and assumed
for v8. The closed loop of Section~\ref{sec:gaintest} does not decide it, and
a hand check would (Section~\ref{sec:valresults}).}

\subsection{Input-Free Angular Momentum Test}
\label{sec:momtest}

\rev{Multiplying \eqref{eq:lin2} by $r$ and adding it to \eqref{eq:lin1}
eliminates the voltage and every back-EMF term:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:momtest}
\bigl(r\beta+M_pl\bigr)\ddot x
=\bigl(I_p+M_pl^2+M_plr\bigr)\ddot\phi-M_pgl\phi .
\end{equation}
This is the angular momentum balance about the wheel contact point of
Remark~\ref{rem:momentum}: the motor torque acts between body and wheels and
drops out, and only gravity remains. With the revision~7 sign of the reaction
the same elimination gives instead
\begin{equation}\label{eq:momrev7}
\bigl(M_pl-r\beta\bigr)\ddot x
=\bigl(I_p+M_pl^2-M_plr\bigr)\ddot\phi-M_pgl\phi .
\end{equation}
In terms of the encoder reading $p=x+r\phi$, \eqref{eq:momtest} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:momenc}
\ddot p=c_1\ddot\phi+c_2\phi ,
\qquad
c_1=\frac{I_p+M_pl^2+M_plr}{r\beta+M_pl}+r ,
\qquad
c_2=-\frac{M_pgl}{r\beta+M_pl},
\end{equation}
and \eqref{eq:momrev7} gives the same form with the coefficients of M2 and M3
in Table~\ref{tab:candidates}. The axle acceleration $\ddot x$ satisfies the
same relation with $c_1-r$ in place of $c_1$, and it can be measured without
the encoder by the accelerometer, corrected for gravity and for its height
above the axle.

The test needs neither the voltage nor $k_m$, $k_e$ or $R$, so the battery
level, PWM scaling, deadzone and motor constants do not enter. It separates the
sign question from the motor-constant question. Because it holds whatever the
motor does, it can be applied to any stretch of data, but it identifies $c_1$
and $c_2$ only where the motion is large compared with the gear backlash, which
sits between the encoder and the wheel. It also requires that no external force
act on the robot other than gravity and the support of the floor at the contact
point: contact with a hand or stand at switch-on, and rolling resistance of the
floor, break it.

\paragraph{Switch-on transients.} Between \SI{0.1}{s} and \SI{0.8}{s} after
switch-on the motions are the largest in the logs: the encoder travels
\SI{39}{mm} and the tilt spans \SI{11}{\degree} in test~4, and \SI{96}{mm} and
\SI{3.9}{\degree} in test~5. The first \SI{0.1}{s} is left out because the
robot may still be touching the hand or stand that held it at its initial lean.
For each candidate, \eqref{eq:momenc} turns the measured tilt history into a
predicted encoder trajectory and a predicted axle trajectory. These are
compared with the logged encoder and with the doubly integrated accelerometer,
with the initial position and velocity and a constant offset fitted per window.
Figure~\ref{fig:pvtransient} shows the result for the v9 runs, and
Table~\ref{tab:momtest} lists all four runs. M1 has the smallest error in every
run and on both sensors, by a factor of 2.5 to 10 against M2 and 3 to 16
against M3. Only tests~4 and~5 are diagnostic on the encoder, however: in
tests~1 and~2 the encoder moves only \SI{5.7}{mm} and \SI{8.2}{mm}, and there
stick-slip in the gearbox can let the candidate with the smallest predicted
coupling win by default. The accelerometer is not behind the gearbox.

Fitting $c_1$ freely, instead of taking it from a candidate, gives $c_1=0.205$
(bootstrap interval 0.12 to 0.25) in test~4 and 0.203 (0.15 to 0.26) in test~5,
against 0.204 for M1 and 0.79 or 1.08 for the $-T$ models. Without the
\SI{7}{ms} IMU correction these become 0.15 and 0.14, so the close agreement
with M1 depends on the correction, while the separation from M2 and M3 does
not. The accelerometer, which the correction does not affect, gives the axle
coefficient $c_1-r=0.189$ (0.169 to 0.210) and 0.242 (0.208 to 0.274) in
tests~4 and~5 (M1: 0.159; M2: 0.741; M3: 1.037). These values are far from
the $-T$ models, but they are 20 to 50\% above M1 with intervals that exclude
it, and in test~5 the value lies above the whole encoder interval ($c_1-r=0.158$,
0.101 to 0.211). Within M1 the axle coefficient depends mainly on $I_p$, and
the test~4 value corresponds to $I_p\approx\SI{0.023}{kg.m^2}$ at the nominal
$l$, against the \SI{0.0169}{kg.m^2} of Table~\ref{tab:phys}. The test~5 value
is not converted, because the same fit gives a gravity coefficient of $+43$ (28
to 59), which no candidate allows. A rolling resistance of the floor would not
explain the excess: in simulation it biases the coefficient low
(Section~\ref{sec:gaintest}). In tests~1 and~2 the fits are mixed: the test~1
accelerometer gives $c_1-r=0.18$ (0.13 to 0.22) and the test~2 encoder
$c_1=0.19$ (0.13 to 0.25), both consistent with M1 and far from M2 and M3,
while the test~1 encoder gives $c_1=0.09$ ($-0.01$ to 0.20) and the test~2
accelerometer $c_1-r=-0.10$ ($-0.20$ to 0.01), a sign no candidate allows. The
test~1 accelerometer is the only accelerometer interval that contains M1, and
it comes from a third event. The gravity coefficient $c_2$ is not identified in
the v9 encoder fits, and where a free fit does identify it (the test~5
accelerometer, the test~1 accelerometer, the test~2 encoder) it is positive,
the wrong sign for every candidate.}

\rev{\paragraph{Steady balancing.} After the first second the robot is in a
stick-slip limit cycle: the encoder stays frozen for several tenths of a second
while the body sways, then jumps by a few counts. During those
intervals $p$ does not follow the wheel, and \eqref{eq:momenc} fails for every
candidate ($R^2$ of 0.05 to 0.11 for the filtered encoder fit). The
accelerometer, which sees the axle directly, gives a $\ddot\phi$ coefficient of
0.173 (0.158 to 0.188) and 0.158 (0.145 to 0.172) in tests~4 and~5 at
\SI{8}{Hz} (predicted $c_1-r$ less the IMU height: 0.139 for M1, 0.721 for M2,
1.017 for M3), closest to M1 but excluding it. Its $\phi$ coefficient, 14.1 in
both runs (11.6 to 19.6 and 11.6 to 17.8), contradicts all three, which predict
$c_2+g=4.2$ for M1 and $-28.9$ for M2 and M3. Gear stiction cannot cause this,
because it acts between body and wheels and drops out of \eqref{eq:momtest}
like the motor torque. An external force is needed, or an error in the
integrated tilt or in $l$. Rolling resistance of the floor is a candidate, but
it has not been shown to fit: in the simulated v9 loop, the 6 to 10\% of the
weight that holds the base in the mirrored frame (Section~\ref{sec:gaintest})
gives a $\phi$ coefficient of 5 to 9 and lowers the $\ddot\phi$ coefficient to
0.05 to 0.08, against 14.1 and 0.16 to 0.17 measured. With the encoder not
mirrored, 2 to 6\% gives a $\phi$ coefficient of 9 to 13, inside the measured
intervals at 6\%, but a $\ddot\phi$ coefficient of only 0.05 to 0.09. The frequency response from the
gyroscope to the encoder rate does not discriminate either, because a
simulated M2 plant with backlash reproduces the low values measured.

\paragraph{Validation of the method.} Every estimator above was first run on
eight simulated closed-loop runs: M1 and M2 plants, clean and with gearbox
friction, \SI{2}{\degree} of backlash and a rolling resistance of 2\% of the
weight, two noise seeds each, passed through the robot's sensor models and
loop. The M2 runs use the gyroscope alone ($\alpha=1$), since with the robot's
filter they fall, and three M2 runs with friction that fell were replaced by
the next seed. On \SIrange{0.1}{0.8}{s} the switch-on test ranked the true
plant first in all 16 run and sensor cases, and the free fit recovered $c_1$
between 0.200 and 0.204 for M1 and between 0.70 and 0.79 for M2.
Figure~\ref{fig:pvcoef} collects the simulated and measured coefficients. The
check has limits. The simulated plants obey the input-free relation by
construction apart from the rolling resistance, they have no IMU delay, and
they use the same IMU height as the analysis. The M2 runs move 300 to
\SI{760}{mm} in the window, against 39 to \SI{96}{mm} on the robot. Over the
longer window \SIrange{0.1}{1.5}{s} the free fit returned $c_1=-0.36$ and 0.27
for the M2 plants with friction, far from their true 0.79, although the ranking
was still right, and on \SIrange{0.1}{0.8}{s} the accelerometer fit is 3 to
12\% low in seven of the eight simulated runs (and unbiased in the eighth). The
bootstrap intervals quoted here are therefore not calibrated 95\% intervals.
With a rolling resistance of 6\% or 10\% of the weight (simulated by the script
of Section~\ref{sec:gaintest}), M1 plants are still ranked first, with the
accelerometer coefficient 6 to 8\% low at 6\% and 25 to 52\% low at 10\%; M2
plants with that much resistance do not balance at all. The stick-slip of the robot is stronger
than the simulated backlash and friction produce, which is why the steady-state
encoder fit works in simulation and not on the logs.}

\subsection{Response to the Voltage}
\label{sec:voltagetest}

\rev{The switch-on transient also tests the input gain, because for at least
the first \SI{60}{ms} the command is saturated at a known \SI{-10}{V} and the
motion is large. It is a consistency check rather than a clean test: the robot
may still have been touching the hand or stand that held it, and the models
start from rest at the logged lean. Each candidate is simulated open loop with
the logged voltages (Figure~\ref{fig:pvstartup}). The cleanest measured
quantity is the body rate from the gyroscope, because the on-board filtered
tilt carries part of the accelerometer's $\ddot x/g$ error during these
accelerations: at \SI{50}{ms} the filtered tilt has fallen by
\SI{5.5}{\degree} and \SI{5.4}{\degree}, while the gyroscope rate,
\SI{-98}{\degree/s} and \SI{-110}{\degree/s}, implies about \SI{2.5}{\degree}.

At \SI{50}{ms} the body is therefore already turning back toward upright. M1
predicts \SI{-139}{\degree/s} and \SI{-168}{\degree/s}. M2 predicts
\SI{+44}{\degree/s} and \SI{+13}{\degree/s}, and M3 \SI{+28}{\degree/s} and
\SI{+5}{\degree/s}: with their tilt input gain, \SI{10}{V} does not overcome
gravity at these angles, and the body is still falling further into its lean.
With the command reversed they fall faster still. At \SI{100}{ms} the measured
rate is \SI{-193}{\degree/s} and \SI{-178}{\degree/s}, against
\SI{-206}{\degree/s} and \SI{-130}{\degree/s} for M1, while M2 and M3 are still
turning the wrong way. The direction excludes the $-T$ models only for $k_m$
near its data-sheet value, however. At nominal $I_p$ and $l$, M3 turns the body
back within \SI{50}{ms} from 2.5 times that $k_m$ in test~4 but from 1.2 times
in test~5, inside the 35\% spread of the data sheets, and M2 from 7.5 and 1.6
times. Reaching the measured rate needs at least 5.4 times for M3 and is
beyond M2 up to 12 times.

The size of the response fixes the tilt input gain of M1 only loosely. Scaling
$k_m$ to fit the filtered tilt over the first \SI{100}{ms} needs a factor of
1.26 and 1.08, but fitting the gyroscope rate at \SI{50}{ms} needs 0.73 and
0.64. The switch-on response therefore bounds the tilt input gain of M1 only to
within about 40\%, in either direction, and contact with the support would slow
the measured rotation further. With $I_p$, $l$ and $k_m$ freed
(\SIrange{0.008}{0.030}{kg.m^2}, \SIrange{0.05}{0.15}{m}, and 0.5 to 5 times)
and the command held at \SI{-10}{V}, M2 can match the rotation over the first
\SI{100}{ms}, whether the target is the filtered tilt, the integrated gyroscope
or the gyroscope rate, with $k_m$ from 1.25 times its data-sheet value in
test~5 (two to three times in test~4) and $l$ of at least \SI{0.08}{m}. It
then predicts the base to move 70 to \SI{135}{mm}, against \SI{51}{mm}
measured, while M1 matches both. That discrimination is the input-free
relation \eqref{eq:momenc} applied to the first \SI{100}{ms}, so it is not
independent of Section~\ref{sec:momtest}. At nominal
parameters the encoder does not discriminate on its own, since the encoder
prediction of M3 (\SI{-47}{mm} and \SI{-48}{mm}) is closer than that of M1
(\SI{-58}{mm}) to the measured \SI{-51}{mm}. This test shares its two events
with Section~\ref{sec:momtest} and with the frame of
Section~\ref{sec:datatests}.

This is how the sign error shows in the voltages: not as a reversed response
to the voltage, but, at the data-sheet motor constant, as a tilt authority too
small to turn the body back against gravity within the first \SI{50}{ms}, which
the robot did.}

\subsection{Gains Used on the Robot}
\label{sec:gaintest}

\rev{The v9 firmware computes the command as a linear function of the logged
on-board estimates, so the gains are recovered by least squares. In the
firmware's frame both v9 runs used
\begin{equation*}
K=\begin{bmatrix}-5.49 & -5.86 & 118.4 & 0.90\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation*}
with an RMS residual of at most \SI{2.4}{mV} ($R^2>0.99999$). The same fit
recovers the v8 gains, $K=[-0.22,\ -0.38,\ K_3,\ 2.61]$ with $K_3=26.9$ in
test~1 and 72.5 in test~2. If the encoder is mirrored, as
Section~\ref{sec:datatests} infers for v9 and assumes for v8, the position and
velocity gains change sign, and all four runs fed back position and velocity
with the opposite sign to the LQR gains designed for this plant, $K=[-0.30,\ -13.4,\ 22.1,\ 3.12]$ on M1 and
$[-0.30,\ -0.90,\ 74.9,\ 11.9]$ on M3. They contribute little to the tilt
loop: at the few millimeters the base moves near upright they contribute tens
of millivolts, against about \SI{2}{V} per degree of tilt in v9 (0.5 and
\SI{1.3}{V} in v8). They set the slow base mode, however: with the v9 gains M1
has a mode at \SI{0.9}{s^{-1}} in the mirrored frame and \SI{0.04}{s^{-1}}
with the encoder not mirrored (without the estimator,
Table~\ref{tab:gaintest}).

Table~\ref{tab:gaintest} gives the least stable mode of each loop on each
candidate. Test~1 drove away for \SI{3}{s}, growing at \SI{1.49}{s^{-1}}, until
the robot was switched off \SI{74}{mm} later. M1 predicts \SI{1.48}{s^{-1}}, M2
\SI{5.48}{s^{-1}} and M3 \SI{2.72}{s^{-1}}. This rate is set mainly by the weak
tilt gain, not by the frame. Without the estimator, the rate of M1 is
\SI{1.44}{s^{-1}} with the position and velocity gains removed and
\SI{1.35}{s^{-1}} with the encoder not mirrored; that of M2 stays above
\SI{5.4}{s^{-1}} in every case, while that of M3 falls to \SI{1.48}{s^{-1}}
with the encoder not mirrored. So test~1 contradicts M2 but separates M1 from
M3 only in the mirrored frame. The rate also depends on $k_e$: with the values
of the motor sweeps scaled to the present gear ratio (0.44 to
\SI{0.50}{V.s/rad}, Section~\ref{sec:predtest}) M1 predicts 1.9 to
\SI{2.0}{s^{-1}}. Test~2 drifted at \SI{1.6}{s^{-1}}, between M1 and M3 (0.65
and 0.75) and M2 (3.89), so M1 and M2 miss it by similar factors; with the
encoder not mirrored the test~2 loop on M3 is stable. The run ended in a
chatter at the \SI{38}{Hz} Nyquist frequency of its loop, at \SI{\pm10}{V},
which no linear model predicts.

With the v9 gains every candidate is linearly unstable at the nominal input
gain, and in the mirrored frame no input-gain multiplier between 0.1 and 10
stabilizes any of them. With the encoder not mirrored, 1.3 to 5 times
stabilizes M1 and 1.3 to 1.5 times M2 (with the estimator), although the
simulated M2 loop still falls. Yet the robot balanced
for \SI{12.6}{s} and \SI{21.2}{s} with the base held within a few millimeters.
The v9 loop was therefore simulated with the logged gains for \SI{21}{s}, using
the sensors, filter, estimator, saturation and PWM truncation of the firmware
(the simulator of the method validation), on each candidate over 160 parameter
sets ($I_p$ from 0.008 to \SI{0.030}{kg.m^2}, $l$ from 0.05 to \SI{0.15}{m},
$k_m$ from 0.5 to 4 times and $k_e$ from 1 to 1.7 times nominal), clean and
with gearbox friction, backlash and rolling resistance, with the encoder
mirrored, not mirrored, and with the tilt gains alone
(Table~\ref{tab:v9sim}). With the robot's filter no M2 plant stayed up longer
than \SI{5.3}{s}. This does not rest on the complementary filter: with the gyroscope alone
($\alpha=1$), the nominal M2 loop falls within 0.7 to \SI{5.5}{s} in every
frame, clean or with friction and backlash. M3 stayed up only with the encoder
not mirrored, and then drove at least \SI{1.9}{m} away. With gearbox friction,
backlash and 2\% rolling resistance, M1 balanced with the base held in 61 of
the 160 sets with the encoder not mirrored and in 115 with the tilt gains
alone, but in only 2 in the mirrored frame, where its \SI{0.9}{s^{-1}} mode
drives the base off. Clean, it held the base in none of the 160 sets in any
frame. M1 therefore holds its base only if the floor resists rolling, in
either frame. For the nominal M1 in the mirrored frame, a resistance of 4\% of
the weight keeps it up with the base wandering \SIrange{23}{28}{mm} over the
last \SI{10}{s}, and 6\% holds the base within \SI{8}{mm}; with the encoder not
mirrored, 2\% holds it within 6 to \SI{10}{mm}. M2 and M3 fall within \SI{3}{s} at
every level from 0 to 15\% in every frame. The frame thus sets how much floor
resistance the v9 runs require, not whether they require it. The floor the
robot ran on was not recorded. The simulation, from
\texttt{scripts/validate\_closed\_loop.py}, writes its results to
\texttt{generated/plant\_closedloop.json}.

This is how the sign error shows in the controller results. With the gains the
robot actually ran, no plant with the revision~7 sign balances with its base
held over the parameter range tried: M2 falls within \SI{5.3}{s} in every set
and frame, and M3 stays up only with the encoder not mirrored, where it drives
at least \SI{1.9}{m} away, while the robot balanced for \SI{21}{s} with its
base held within a few millimeters. Only M1 balances with its base held, and
only if the floor resists rolling, by about 2\% of the weight with the encoder
not mirrored and about 6\% in the frame inferred from the switch-on transients.
In the simulated loop that resistance does not reproduce the steady-balancing
coefficients of Section~\ref{sec:momtest}, so the two do not yet support each
other. The closed loop therefore confirms the sign but does not decide the
frame. The floor resistance and the encoder frame are what the logs leave open
about how the v9 runs held their base; two bench checks settle them
(Section~\ref{sec:valresults}).

\subsection{Input Regression, Prediction and Motor Data}
\label{sec:predtest}

\rev{Regressing $\ddot\phi$ on the states and the voltage over the switch-on
transients does not settle $\mathcal{B}_4$. With the command acting in the same
loop, tests~1, 2, 4 and~5 give 8.2, 1.2, 2.5 and 9.5 per volt; with the command
acting in the next loop they give $-0.3$, 2.8, 4.2 and 6.7. With the
same-loop timing two runs point toward M1 and two toward M2; with the next-loop
timing three are nearer M2 and the fourth lies midway. Tests~1 and~2 with the
command in the same loop also give a negative ratio
$\mathcal{B}_p/\mathcal{B}_4$ ($-0.09$ and $-0.42$), which no candidate
allows. The bootstrap has only about three blocks per window, and several
estimates fall outside their own intervals, so no interval is quoted. $k_e$ is
not identifiable from the back-EMF term. The regression is confined to the
transients because elsewhere the command is nearly a linear function of the
states.

As a predictor of the base after switch-on, M1 is best in the v9 runs: over
\SI{100}{ms} and \SI{200}{ms} from the logged states and voltages in test~4
its RMS encoder error is \SI{2.4}{mm} and \SI{5.3}{mm}, against 6.3 and 16.9
for M2, 9.2 and 27.4 for M3, and 9.9 and 18.5 for holding the state. In the v8
runs, with a few millimeters of motion, M2 is better (\SI{0.54}{mm} and
\SI{1.14}{mm} against 0.80 and 1.83 in test~1; 1.50 and 3.95 against 2.11 and
5.05 in test~2), and in steady balancing M2 predicts the base better than M1 in
both v9 runs. These comparisons do not discriminate, however: there the base
barely moves, M2 predicts less base motion per volt, and holding the state or
extrapolating at constant velocity does about as well or better (in test~2,
holding the state gives \SI{1.15}{mm} and \SI{1.44}{mm}). For the tilt,
holding the state beats every candidate. In steady balancing M2 and M3 predict
the tilt about three times better than M1 in test~5 (\SI{0.22}{\degree} and
\SI{0.20}{\degree} against \SI{0.61}{\degree} at \SI{100}{ms}); in test~4 M3
is slightly better than M1 and M2 slightly worse (0.61, 0.65 and
\SI{0.65}{\degree}). This comparison does not discriminate either. In the
simulated v9 loop with gearbox friction, backlash and floor resistance, a
plant that is M1 is also predicted better by M2 and M3 (0.25 to
\SI{0.30}{\degree} against 0.42 to \SI{0.50}{\degree} at \SI{100}{ms}), in
either frame, because small commands barely move the wheels through the
backlash and stiction and the larger input gain of M1 overstates their
effect.

The motor sweeps of \texttt{Tests/Motor Tests} (test~9) give
$k_e=\SIrange{0.49}{0.57}{V.s/rad}$, assuming \SI{12}{V} at full PWM, with
fitted deadzones of \SIrange{1.9}{2.4}{V} and, for one motor,
$R=\SI{2.4}{\ohm}$ (the fitted $R$ of the other motor is negative). They were
recorded on the earlier 34:1 gearmotors, and no torque was logged, so $k_m$
cannot be found from them. Scaled by the gear ratio they still exceed the
$k_e$ of M1, \SI{0.307}{V.s/rad}, by 40 to 65\%. Since the motors differ, they
confirm only the order of magnitude of $k_e$ in M1 and M2; the value
\SI{0.00361}{V.s/rad} of M3 is two orders too small.}

\subsection{Conclusions and Remaining Measurements}
\label{sec:valresults}

\rev{Would the sign error show in the voltages and in the controller results?
Not as a reversed response: in all three candidates a positive voltage pushes
the base forward and turns the body backward. It shows in the size of the
response, and the logs measure it in three places
(Table~\ref{tab:verdict}). First, at switch-on, with the command saturated at
\SI{-10}{V}, the gyroscope reads \SI{-98}{\degree/s} and \SI{-110}{\degree/s}
after \SI{50}{ms}: the body is already turning back toward upright. M1
predicts \SI{-139}{\degree/s} and \SI{-168}{\degree/s}, while M2 and M3 have
the body still falling further into its lean (\SI{+44}{\degree/s} and
\SI{+13}{\degree/s}, \SI{+28}{\degree/s} and \SI{+5}{\degree/s}); in both runs
they turn it back only with $k_m$ at least 2.5 times its data-sheet value.
Second, per unit of body rotation, the base moved about three to five times
less than M2 and M3 predict, on the encoder and on the accelerometer, in a
relation that involves neither the voltage nor the motor constants; on the two
v9 switch-on transients M1 predicts the motion 4 to 16 times better than M2
and M3. The encoder agrees with M1 in the frame inferred from these same
transients, while the accelerometer reads 20 to 50\% above M1. Third, with the
test~1 gains the base drove away at \SI{1.49}{s^{-1}}; M1 predicts 1.48 (1.9
to 2.0 with the $k_e$ of the motor sweeps) and M2 more than 5.4 in every
encoder frame, while M3 predicts 2.72, or 1.48 with the encoder not mirrored,
so this run separates M1 from M3 only in the inferred frame. The
\SI{1.6}{s^{-1}} drift of test~2 is missed by M1 and M2 by similar factors.
The first two come from the same two switch-on events, the third from a third
run. With the v9 gains no linear model is stable in the inferred frame, so
those runs speak only through the simulated loop, where no plant with the
revision~7 sign balances with its base held over the parameter range tried:
with the robot's filter M2 falls within \SI{5.3}{s}, and M3 stays up only with
the encoder not mirrored, then driving at least \SI{1.9}{m} away, while the robot balanced
for \SI{21}{s} with its base held within a few millimeters. This confirms the
reaction sign of \eqref{eq:pendm} by measurement, independently of the
momentum argument of Remark~\ref{rem:momentum}. The 26 unanalysed v7 logs are
the natural further replication.

The tests do not validate M1 quantitatively:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item the axle coefficient measured by the accelerometer exceeds that of M1 by
20 to 50\%, with intervals that exclude it; in test~4 this corresponds to an
$I_p$ of about \SI{0.023}{kg.m^2}, but the test~5 fit also has a gravity
coefficient of the wrong sign, so the excess may come from an unmodelled force
rather than from $I_p$;
\item the steady-balancing coefficients contradict all three models, and the
floor resistance that the closed loop needs does not reproduce them;
\item in the simulated loop M1 holds the base of the v9 runs (range under
\SI{10}{mm}) only if the floor resisted rolling, by about 6\% of the weight in
the encoder frame inferred from the switch-on transients and about 2\% with
the encoder not mirrored; the resistance was not measured, so the closed loop
does not decide the frame;
\item the switch-on response bounds the tilt input gain of M1 only to within
about 40\%: the gyroscope rate at \SI{50}{ms} asks for 0.64 to 0.73 times the
data-sheet $k_m$, the filtered tilt for 1.08 to 1.26 times;
\item in tests~1 and~2 only two of the four switch-on fits agree with M1 on
$c_1$, and both, like the test~5 accelerometer fit, give a gravity coefficient
of the wrong sign;
\item M2 and M3 predict the tilt near upright better than M1 in test~5, and M2
predicts the base better in tests~1 and~2 and in steady balancing, but the
simulator shows that M2 and M3 also predict the tilt of an M1 plant with
backlash and friction better than M1 does, and that near upright holding the
state predicts as well, so these comparisons do not discriminate;
\item the drift of test~2 is missed by M1 and M2 by similar factors.
\end{itemize}

The same data show the limits of all three linear models. Near upright the
robot is dominated by gear backlash and stick-slip, by an external force such
as friction with the floor, and possibly by a motor deadzone (about \SI{2}{V} on the earlier gearmotors,
not measured on the present ones), none of which the models contain. These
effects are what the uncertainty terms $d_1$ and $d_2$ of \eqref{eq:plantform}
must absorb on the real robot, and they bound how well any model-based
estimator can predict the tilt over \SI{100}{ms} or more.

The parameters themselves are less certain than the sign. $I_p$ and $l$ come
from a lumped-mass idealization, and $k_m$ from data sheet values that differ by
35\%. The input-free test does not depend on them much: over
$I_p\in[0.010,0.025]\,\si{kg.m^2}$ and $l\in[0.065,0.085]\,\si{m}$, the $c_1$ of
M1 stays between 0.16 and 0.25, while that of M2 stays above 0.40. The $c_1$ of
M2 falls with larger $l$, to 0.29 at $I_p=\SI{0.010}{kg.m^2}$ and
$l=\SI{0.12}{m}$, outside the v9 encoder intervals but with an axle
coefficient, 0.25, inside the test~5 accelerometer interval, and to 0.24,
inside the v9 encoder intervals, only at $I_p=\SI{0.005}{kg.m^2}$, less than a
third of the lumped-mass value. The switch-on response, tilt and encoder
together, and the closed loop exclude M2 over the whole range tried; the
direction of the body rate at \SI{50}{ms} alone excludes the $-T$ models, at
nominal $I_p$ and $l$, only for $k_m$ below 2.5 times its data-sheet value in
test~4 and 1.2 times in test~5. A few bench measurements would complete the validation
(Table~\ref{tab:benchtests}). The swing period of the body with the wheels
clamped gives $I_p$ at a measured $l$. Free-run and locked-rotor tests give
$k_e$, $k_m$, $R$ and the reflected armature inertia. Blocking the wheels and
applying \SI{1}{V} is a direct, model-free check of the reaction sign, since
the body must pitch opposite to the way the wheels would roll. Rolling the
robot by hand with the motors off fixes the encoder frame, and the force needed
to push it slowly over the floor it balanced on gives the rolling resistance;
together they decide how the v9 runs held their base.}
